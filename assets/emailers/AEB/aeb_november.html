Crack into the Nutritional Power of Eggs
It’s no secret that eggs are healthy. But did you know these facts about eggs? Share with your patients and clients so they can see how eggs can help them get Stronger by the Dozen.
  • Food for Thought: Choline is a crucial nutrient for supporting brain development, memory and mood, and one that 90% of Americans don't get enough of.1,2 Eating two-eggs any time of day provides 50-70% of your daily choline requirement.3
  • Egg-cellent News: Eggs contribute lutein and zeaxanthin to the diet, antioxidants that are also found in fruits and vegetables.4 Emerging research indicates there may also be a connection between lutein and cognitive function.5
  • Muscle Fuel: One of nature’s most perfect proteins, each egg contains 6 grams of complete protein, including all nine essential amino acids in a bioavailable form.6 This makes them one of the highest-quality protein sources.6

Eggs: A Grocery Cart Staple
Grocery shopping can be stressful if you do not have a few go-to foods. In this Delish.com article, RD partner Lauren Manaker shares how eggs always have a spot in her grocery cart for their meal prep versatility and nutritional power. A complete protein with essential vitamins and minerals like folate (6% DV), vitamins A (8% DV), and D (6% DV), and an excellent source of B12 and choline.1 Eating two eggs can provide 50 to 70 percent of the daily choline requirement, a nutrient that helps support memory and mood.1,2

How a Clinical Psychologist Reduces the Stress of Meal Prep
When it comes to meal prep, reducing decision fatigue can simplify your days and alleviate stress, according to Dr. Rachel Goldman, PhD. She recommends preparing simple options with eggs in advance, making it easier to enjoy a healthy meal or snack without overthinking it. Try her favorite hack: meal prep a batch of hard-boiled eggs and enjoy them in different ways throughout the week—like mashing them with cottage cheese and avocado to top on toast, or paired with fruit and nuts for a quick snack plate.

New Study Alert: Food Allergy Improvements with Early Introduction
Leading allergy organizations continue to reinforce that egg and peanut should be introduced around 6 months of life but not before 4 months.7 Eggs are also an important first food for babies to help meet essential nutrient needs.8-11
A new study published in Pediatrics found a significant decreased risk of peanut or any (IgE) food allergy incidence among younger children (0-3 years) following publication of early introduction guidelines.12 This is a significant finding because it suggests that public health guidelines are working to help prevent food allergy.
Find more resources including current guidelines, recipes and tips for early introduction at IncredibleEgg.org.
